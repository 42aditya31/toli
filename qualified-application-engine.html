Part 02

About your 250 number — I was wrong

In my first version I said 250 applications per employee per day was not possible. You told me it is. You are right and I was wrong. Here is the mistake I made, because it turns out to be important.

What I got wrong

I assumed every application needs its own job search. Like this:

My wrong maths
Find a job = 0.6 minutes
Apply to it = 1.0 minute
Total = 1.6 minutes per application
359 working minutes ÷ 1.6 = 224... and with harder forms mixed in, about 151 a day

What you actually do

Your employee finds one job, then applies to that same job for all five candidates. So the search time is shared:

The correct maths

Find a job once = 0.6 minutes
Apply for candidate 1 = 1.0 minute
Apply for candidate 2 = 1.0 minute
Apply for candidate 3 = 1.0 minute
Apply for candidate 4 = 1.0 minute
Apply for candidate 5 = 1.0 minute
Total = 5.6 minutes for 5 applications

5.6 ÷ 5 = 1.12 minutes per application
359 working minutes ÷ 1.12 = 320 applications a day

So 250 a day is not only possible — you have room to spare. My number was wrong because I forgot that one search can serve five people.

Now the important part: what makes 250 possible is also what breaks it

Look again at how the speed is achieved. Five different people apply to the same job. Think about what that means:

  1. Five people from your company apply to one job. The person reading applications at that company sees five applications that look similar, arriving together. That looks like spam, even if every person is good.
  2. Your own candidates compete with each other. If the job really fits all five, they now fight each other for one position. You are working against your own clients.
  3. If the job does not fit all five, four are wasted. And a job almost never fits five different people equally well.
  4. One minute per application means nobody reads the job. There is no time to read the description, no time to change the CV, no time to think. It is copy and paste.
  5. It only works on one-click apply. A real company form takes 5–6 minutes. So this method traps you on LinkedIn and Indeed — exactly the places that are most crowded.
The real finding

Your number is honest. Your method is the problem. You are fast because you are not choosing jobs for each person — you are sending the same job to everyone. Speed came from removing the thinking, and the thinking is the part the candidate is paying for.

In simple words

You built a very fast machine. But the machine is fast because it skipped the part that makes applications work. The fix is not to go slower. The fix is to let a computer do the thinking — find and check jobs for each person separately — and let your employee do only the final click. Then each application is chosen for one person, and you are still fast.

One note before you read on: because 250 a day is real, your team has spare capacity you did not know about. At 1.12 minutes each, 250 applications take 280 minutes, and your employee has 359. That extra 79 minutes a day, plus everything AI takes over, is where the new system comes from.

Part 03

Why sending many applications stopped working

Your business model made sense in 2022. Three things changed since then. All three are bad for "send many applications".

1. Everyone got fast, so being fast is no longer special

ChatGPT and similar tools let any job seeker apply to 30 jobs in the time it used to take to apply to one. So everyone started doing it. The numbers:

  • LinkedIn now receives about 11,000 applications every minute. That is 45% more than one year ago.
  • Companies now get about 244 applications for one open job.
  • Greenhouse (software companies use to collect applications) reported that the number of applications needed per hire went up 157% since 2022.

When you were one of the few applying fast, speed was your advantage. Now speed is what everybody has. It stopped being an advantage and became the normal background noise.

Companies noticed and started fighting back. In August 2026 LinkedIn began showing a warning to people when their profile does not match a job. That warning exists to stop exactly the behaviour you sell.

2. The maths now favours the opposite strategy

Research on real applications in 2026 shows a clear pattern:

  • A generic application (same CV sent everywhere) gets an interview about 2–3% of the time. At the very low-effort end — one-click, no reading — it is closer to 1%.
  • An application that was actually written for that job gets an interview 7–9% of the time.

So fewer good applications beat many weak ones. Here is what that looks like for one candidate over one month:

Read that table slowly, because it is the whole reason to change:

70% fewer applications. 40% less work for your employee. About 50% more interviews.

Even if you think 5% is too hopeful and the real number is 3.5%, you still get the same interviews as now, using less than half the work. There is no version of this where 50 weak applications a day is the better choice.

3. The websites you depend on do not want you there

LinkedIn's rules forbid software that applies automatically or copies data. This is not new, but the enforcement got much harder:

  • In 2025 and 2026 LinkedIn stopped giving warnings and started suspending accounts on the first offence.
  • In March 2026 LinkedIn removed the company page of an automation tool called HeyReach and banned the founder's personal profile.

Indeed is not a safe alternative either. Indeed closed its public job-data service to new users in October 2022 and shut down the old one in 2023–24. Today only a few approved partner companies get access, and only for employer-side features.

The danger you are carrying right now

If your team uses candidates' LinkedIn accounts to apply quickly, one enforcement wave does two things at the same time: you cannot deliver your service any more, and your clients lose their LinkedIn profile — the most important professional account they have. That is not just a bad day. That is your product breaking and your customers being harmed together, with your name on it.

4. Your price is stuck in the worst place

Right now you compete in two directions at once, and you lose in both:

Who you compete withWhat they chargeWhy you lose
Software tools (LazyApply, Simplify, Jobright)$99–999 per year · $15 per month · $18 per weekThey sell the same thing you sell — many applications — and software costs them almost nothing. You pay salaries. You cannot win a price fight

You are in the middle: people expect software prices, but you pay human costs. The way out is not a price cut. It is to move up — sell fit, proof and results, which no $99 browser tool can offer.

Part 04

Problems you did not mention

You listed the obvious pains: messy sheets, manual assignment, no clear view. Those are real and this document fixes them. These are the ones that were not on your list, starting with the ones that cost the most money.

The number of good jobs runs out before your employees do

You plan capacity by asking "how fast can a person fill forms?" But for most candidates the real limit comes much earlier: there are not 50 good jobs a day for them.

For a specific candidate, "50 a day" can only be reached by applying to jobs they are not right for. That lowers their chance of an interview, uses up companies they might genuinely fit later, and now triggers LinkedIn's "you don't match this job" warning. Your promise forces your team to hurt your own client.

Your most specific candidates probably lose you money

Specific candidates take the most employee time — harder to find jobs for, longer forms at senior level, more care needed — but usually pay the same price as an easy candidate. You almost certainly have candidates costing you more than they pay, and no way to see which ones. Part 17 turns this into a real number.

You are throwing away the most valuable thing you produce

Every day your team creates thousands of small facts like this:

this person + this job + applied on this date + through this website
→ company replied / stayed silent / rejected / gave an interview

Nobody else has this. Job boards do not know who got hired. Software tools do not follow up. You do this work every day and the result disappears the moment the employee closes the browser tab.

If you saved it, after about a year you could tell a candidate: "people like you, applying to jobs like this, get a reply 11% of the time." No browser extension can copy that, because the value is in your recorded results, not in clever code. The information you are not saving is worth more than the work that creates it.

You cannot tell if an employee did the work

Your team types their own numbers into a sheet. If someone writes 250 and actually did 80, you would never find out. This is not about trusting your people. It is about the system: when the person being measured also writes the measurement, the number always drifts, and always in one direction. Part 12 makes this impossible rather than trying to police it.

You are mixing two opposite kinds of "candidate left"

A candidate who leaves because they got a job is your best advertisement. A candidate who leaves because nothing happened is a refund risk and a bad review. If both are just "closed" in your sheet, you cannot tell success from failure, and a quality problem can hide for months. Use three separate words from day one: got a job, gave up, time finished.

Your one-time price has no end date

You said price depends on what the candidate prefers — monthly or one-time. The problem with one-time: the candidate reasonably expects you to keep working until they get a job. But your costs are monthly. If someone takes six months, you work for free from month three. And the people who take longest are the specific candidates who already cost you the most. This leak grows quietly and you cannot see it.

You are one person doing three jobs

You are the owner, the developer, and the operations manager. The build plan in Part 22 is arranged around that. But be honest with yourself: you cannot build this system and run the daily operation at the same time past about 50 candidates. Part 16 gives you the exact moment to hire, and it is not another person to apply to jobs.

Part 05

The big change

Three changes, in this order. Everything else in this document is just details of these three.

Change 1 — Sell a different thing

Stop counting "applications". Start counting good applications. A good application must be all four of these:

RuleWhat it means
It fitsThe computer gave it a match score above your minimum. Not the employee's opinion — the system's score
It is cleanIt passed all the safety checks: not a duplicate, right city, right level, nothing invented
It was deliveredIt went into the company's own application system
It has proofA confirmation email or receipt exists and is saved

This one definition does a surprising amount of work. It makes the service provable to the candidate. It makes the employee target impossible to fake. It gives the AI a clear job. And it changes the conversation with candidates from "how many?" to "how good?".

Change 2 — Apply on the company's own website instead of LinkedIn

This is the main strategic change. Instead of racing 244 other people through LinkedIn, apply directly on the company's careers page — which goes straight into the system the recruiter actually reads.

What "ATS" means

An ATS is the software a company uses to collect job applications. The common ones are Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby and Workday. When you see a careers page on a company website, you are usually looking at one of these.

The useful part: Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby publish their job lists openly, for free, with no password needed. They want people to find these jobs. So you can legally and safely read them — unlike LinkedIn.

Tie the price to how many good jobs actually exist for that person, and put a time limit on every deal.

Two details that matter more than the prices:

  • The one-time option becomes a prepaid fixed period — for example 12 weeks, 15% cheaper. Never "until you get a job". This removes your biggest hidden cost.
  • Write a "not enough jobs" rule into the contract. Something like: "If fewer than N suitable jobs exist for you in a week, we apply to every one that does, give you credit for the rest, and show you which jobs we rejected and why."
Why that second rule is clever

Your biggest weakness is that good jobs run out. Every competitor has this problem and every competitor hides it. If you tell the truth about it, that weakness becomes the reason people trust you. The candidate portal in Part 14 shows this automatically, so it costs you nothing to be honest.

Do this before writing any code

Changes 1 and 3 are business decisions. They cost nothing and take one afternoon. Make them this week. If you build software around the old "50 per day" promise, you will bake a number you are about to abandon into your database, your employee targets and your reports — and pulling it out later is painful.

Part 07

What information to store

Get this right and questions like "why did applications drop?" become easy. Get it wrong and no dashboard will help you.

Five rules first

  1. Never overwrite a status. Add a new line instead. If an application goes from "sent" to "rejected", do not replace the word — write a new row saying "rejected, at this time". Six months later you can still see the whole story. If you overwrite, the history is gone forever.
  2. Save what you used, when you used it. Save the match score, the CV version and the answers as they were at that moment. If you look them up fresh later, you will never be able to tell whether your matching got better.
  3. Identify companies by their website address, not their name. "Acme", "Acme Inc." and "ACME Corporation" never match as text. acme.com always does. This is what makes "do not apply twice to the same company" actually work.
  4. Numbers must come from what the system saw, never from what a person typed. If a person can type it, it is not a measurement — it is an opinion.
  5. Record AI cost against the candidate from your very first AI call. One extra column. Without it, "what does each candidate cost me?" can never be answered, and Part 17 depends on it.

Cleaning duplicates is where quality is won

The same job appears on the company page, on two job boards and on a partner site. Duplicates are not a small annoyance — they are how you end up applying twice to the same company and embarrassing your client. Four things to do:

  • One company = one website address. Build a list of alternative names that point to the same address.
  • One job = company + cleaned title + city. Use a fingerprint of the text to separate genuinely different jobs at the same company.
  • Clean up titles. "Sr. SWE II", "Senior Software Engineer" and "Software Engineer (Senior)" are the same thing. A cheap AI call plus a saved list handles this — the same titles come up again and again, so you only pay once.
  • Watch the age. Jobs older than about 3 weeks convert much worse. Record when it was posted and lower the score as it gets older.
The channel almost nobody uses

The thing that works best of all is not an application. It is sending a short message directly to the hiring manager soon after a job appears on the company's own page. It works several times better than any form, and it is completely outside the crowded competition. It is late in the plan only because it needs the rest of the system first — but do not forget it exists. It is your premium plan.

Part 09

How to match jobs to people

Do not build machine learning. Build a funnel of filters that get more expensive as fewer jobs survive, so the expensive AI step only ever looks at about 60 jobs per person per day.

The most important line in this table

Step 1 removes 93% of the jobs and costs nothing.

Every rule you can write as a simple database question is a rule you never pay AI to think about. Resist the temptation to "let the AI figure it out" — it costs about 100× more and is worse at exactly these simple checks.

What the AI should give back

Ask for a structured answer, not just a number:

score 0 to 100
sub-scores required skills · level · industry · location and visa · salary · career direction · company type
deal-breakers for example: needs security clearance, office only in wrong city
evidence for each sub-score: a quote from the job advert and a quote from the CV
hints what the CV should emphasise for this job
confidence 0 to 1

The evidence part is the clever bit and most people skip it. By forcing the AI to quote the actual words from the job advert and the actual words from the CV, it cannot make up a reason. It also gives you three free things: your employee trusts the list, the candidate sees "why this job" in their portal, and you can check the AI when a score looks wrong.

A score with no explanation is a score you can never improve.

Which AI to use, and what it costs

ModelPrice per million tokensUse it for

Two tricks cut this bill roughly in half. Use both from day one:

  • Batch requests. Scoring runs at 2am for a list used at 9am, so it does not need to be fast. Sending it as a batch costs 50% less. Free money.
  • Prompt caching. You score 60 jobs against the same candidate profile. That profile is the same text 60 times. Caching it means you only pay full price once. Note it only works if the profile text is long enough (the minimum varies by model), so check that caching is actually happening rather than assuming.

All together — scoring, CV adjusting, safety checks and reading emails — expect about $0.45 per candidate per day, or $8–15 per month. Set a monthly alert per candidate anyway, so a mistake in your code that doubles usage wakes you up instead of surprising you in the bill.

The part that becomes your real advantage

At first your score is just a sensible guess. But after about 2,000 applications where you recorded what happened, you can compare: which scores actually led to a reply? Then adjust. At that point the number changes meaning completely — from "the AI thinks this is a 73" to "people like you applying to jobs like this get a reply 11% of the time."

That is genuinely useful to a candidate, and no competitor can copy it, because the value is in your recorded results, not in the code.

Be realistic about timing: this needs Part 12 running first and several months of data. Build the simple score now; this comes later.

Part 18

Safety and law

You handle CVs, personal details, maybe passwords, and you act in other people's names. Your candidates are in several countries, so several sets of rules apply at once. Two items here can genuinely end the company.

Danger 1 — holding candidates' passwords

If you keep candidates' LinkedIn or Indeed passwords, that is your single biggest risk. It breaks those websites' rules, it makes you responsible for your client's most important professional account, and one infected employee laptop exposes every candidate at once.

Worse: using several candidates' accounts from one computer links those accounts together in the platform's eyes. That is the pattern that gets them all banned together.

The goal: no candidate passwords anywhere in your system. Applying on company websites needs none. If a login is truly unavoidable, use a browser extension the candidate installs themselves and can remove at any time — never a stored password. While you are still changing over: use a proper password vault (not your database), where the employee never sees the actual password and every use is logged.

Danger 2 — applying as someone else

You submit applications in your candidate's name. That needs a signed permission form for each candidate, saying what you may send, as whom, on which websites, and with what claims. Store it and link it to every application record.

Many application forms have a box saying "I confirm this information is true." Your permission form must clearly cover the candidate letting you tick that box — and your invented-facts check must make sure it is never ticked falsely. Without both, one wrong answer stops being a mistake and becomes a false statement.

Privacy, across several countries at once

Because you serve Europe, the US and Asia, use GDPR (the European rule) as your baseline. It is the strictest, so following it mostly covers the others too. In practice:

  • Reason for holding data — for the core service it is "we need it to do the job you paid for". For anything extra, ask permission separately.
  • Sensitive information — CVs often contain nationality, health, religion or photos. Detect it when reading the CV, keep it out of AI prompts where it is not needed, and never let it affect a matching decision.
  • Candidate rights — they can ask to see their data, correct it, delete it, or take it elsewhere, and you have 30 days. Build the export and delete functions early. Adding deletion later, across a history log, is genuinely painful.
  • How long you keep things — delete CVs and application content 12 months after the candidate leaves. Keep the anonymous totals forever. Your learning data does not need names.
  • Your suppliers — keep a list (hosting, AI provider, job data, email) and have a data agreement with each. Tell candidates who they are.
  • Your employees — each signs a data agreement and a confidentiality agreement. Employees in other countries count as suppliers in legal terms.
  • Where the data lives — decide before you write your first database table. If you will have many European candidates, their data should sit on European servers. Choosing the region at the start is free. Splitting a live database later is a project.
  • India has the DPDP Act 2023 and California has CCPA/CPRA. Both are largely covered if you follow the above.
  • If data leaks, GDPR gives you 72 hours to report it. Write the plan now, not during the emergency.

The EU AI law — probably not your problem, with one warning

Europe's AI Act treats "AI used for hiring" as high risk. But that rule is aimed at companies using AI to choose between candidates. You do the opposite — you choose jobs for a person. That is outside the rule. Also, the strict parts were pushed back from August 2026 to 2 December 2027.

Three things that do apply:

  • The transparency rules started on 2 August 2026 and are being enforced now.
  • If you ever build scoring that ranks candidates — deciding which clients get more effort — that is automatic decision-making about people, and you should get advice first.
  • Never advertise your matching system as a tool for employers to screen candidates. That one sentence in your marketing would pull you into the strict rules and a compliance process you cannot afford.

None of this is legal advice. It is a map of where to spend one hour with a lawyer. With candidates in several countries, one hour is cheap.

Who can see what

  • Four levels: candidate (only their own data), employee (only their assigned candidates, no passwords, no bulk download), team leader (their group), owner (everything plus the log).
  • Enforce this in the database itself, not only in your code. One forgotten line in one page should not be able to show another candidate's CV.
  • CVs in private storage with links that expire. No public folders, ever — badly configured storage is the most common way small companies leak data.
  • Log who viewed what, downloaded what, and changed permissions. Alert specially on bulk downloads — an employee downloading 200 CVs is either leaving or has been hacked.

What gets accounts banned

The specific patterns, so you can avoid them:

  • Any automatic action on LinkedIn — searching, applying, connecting, messaging
  • Server IP addresses and automated-browser signatures
  • Identical answer text and identical timing across many accounts
  • One computer or internet connection used for several candidate accounts — this links them, and bans spread across the whole group
  • Activity at hours that make no sense for the account's stated location
  • CAPTCHA-solving services and IP hiding — this is the line between aggressive and dishonest

Put the rules in your code, not in a document. The "automation rule" field on each job source from Part 07 is read by the sending code, so a source marked "people only" is simply unreachable by automation. Rules in a document get broken. Rules in code do not.

You listed eight AI agents. I want to push back, because building eight of them alone is one of the most reliable ways to lose six months.

What "agent" actually means

An agent is an AI that decides its own steps — it thinks, picks a tool, looks at the result, thinks again, and stops when it decides it is done.

A pipeline is when you decide the steps and AI just does one specific job inside each one.

Agents are right when you cannot predict the steps in advance. Pipelines are right when the same thing happens thousands of times a day — because they are cheaper, they give the same answer every time, and you can explain to a candidate exactly why something happened.

Layer 2 — Work out why, do not ask AI to guess

"Why did applications drop this week?" should never be answered by AI opinion. Calculate it. Total applications is just a few things multiplied together, so you can check each one:

total = active candidates × working days × applications per candidate per day

so a drop comes from one or more of
· fewer active candidates (someone finished or left)
· fewer working days (sickness, holiday)
· each employee doing less (overloaded, harder forms)
· fewer good jobs available
· more applications blocked by checks

Show the list in order of size, then let AI write the sentence. Like this:

"Applications fell 18%. 11 points of that was three candidates finishing their plan, 5 points was fewer senior backend jobs being posted, 2 points was more blocks after Tuesday's duplicate-check change."

That is useful. "It looks like activity was lower this week" is not.

Part 24

If I were you, this is what I would build

One system, on one database, where no number is ever typed by a person. Everything below comes from that one idea.

What it is

One website with three doors — one for you, one for your employees, one for candidates. Behind all three, one database.

Every night, the system downloads jobs from company career pages, removes duplicates, throws out the ones that are impossible for each person, scores the rest with AI, and puts a sorted list on each employee's screen before they start work. Every application saves proof. Every reply lands in your email system, gets read automatically, and becomes a record. Those records produce every number you use to run the business.

How your employees work

They open their screen and see one job. The job, why it matched, a prepared CV, filled-in answers. They click Send or Skip. They never search for a job, never retype an answer, never open a spreadsheet, and never touch a candidate's password — because there isn't one any more. Their numbers are the same numbers you see. They handle 11 candidates instead of 5, and later 20.

How candidates experience it

They upload a CV. AI fills in their profile and they just correct it. They answer 25 questions once. Then they watch: every application, with the company, the date, the confirmation, and — the thing nobody else shows — why that job was chosen for them. When there are not enough good jobs, they are told honestly and given credit. Your weakest point becomes the reason they trust you.

What AI does, and does not do

AI finds, sorts, writes drafts and checks. People decide, click send, and talk. Two real agents; everything else is a fixed pipeline. AI never states a fact about a candidate that is not in their confirmed profile, and never touches LinkedIn.

What you see

One screen showing only what needs your decision today. A Monday summary that explains changes with actual calculations, not guesses. Underneath: cost and profit per candidate, how many more candidates you can take, and a sorted list of where employee time actually went last week.

Words I used that may be unfamiliar

ATS
The software a company uses to collect job applications. Common ones: Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workday. When you apply on a company's careers page, you are usually using one.
API
A way for one program to ask another program for information, without a human clicking anything.
Agent
An AI that decides its own steps. Different from a pipeline, where you decide the steps.
Pipeline
A fixed sequence of steps that you designed. AI does one specific job inside each step.
Token
How AI usage is measured and charged. Roughly three-quarters of a word.
Prompt caching
When you send the same text to AI many times, you pay full price once and much less afterwards.
Batch
Sending many AI requests together, accepting a slower answer, for 50% off.
Embedding / similarity search
Turning text into numbers so a computer can find "things that mean roughly the same" without exact word matching.
Row-level security
The database itself refuses to show a person data that is not theirs — so a mistake in your code cannot leak it.
GDPR
The European privacy law. The strictest one, so following it mostly covers the others.
ToS
Terms of Service. The rules a website makes you agree to. Breaking them can get accounts banned.
Conversion rate
Out of 100 applications, how many turn into the next step (a reply, or an interview).
